\begin{abstract} Although land use regulation remains central to urban development outcomes, local review and approval of development proposals remains a black box. In this paper we assess local regulatory practice using the complete written record of the Los Angeles City Planning Commission from 2018 to 2026, applying large language models to convert 29,000 pages of agendas, minutes, and public correspondence into a structured dataset of 818 development decisions. We show that outright denial of development is rare (1.2\% of all cases), and that the more salient margin is delay. We then investigate factors that predict delay. Surprisingly, all things equal, the physical characteristics of the project have little explanatory power. Instead, public opposition and process-based frictions such as how unusual a case is, or when hearing agenda order, matter more. To measure unusualness, we develop a measure of atypicality based on the text embeddings of proposal summaries. We find that a one standard deviation increase in atypicality is associated with a \gn{AtypicalityCoef} increase in the log odds of delay. Notably, the association is stronger when there are fewer members who have previously heard. Furthermore, atypicality only predicts delay, not eventual outcome, thus reinforcing the interpretation that the delays stem from process-based frictions rather than latent proposal quality. The results provide rare field evidence that bureaucratic capacity, not statutory rules alone, shapes the pace of urban development. \\

\noindent{\textit{Keywords}: local land-use regulation, bureaucratic efficiency} \\
\noindent {\textit{JEL} Classification: D73, R14, R38, R52} 
\end{abstract}
